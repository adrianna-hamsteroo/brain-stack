# Brain Stack: the research behind the timings

Compiled October 2026. Every number below comes from the cited study; where a
detail could only be confirmed from secondary sources, it is listed under
"Not verified". This is a summary for a self-care app, not medical advice.

## What "brain stacking" is

A TikTok trend from 2025–26 (usually credited to the creator @mandyrulez):
you build a "stack" of screen-free activities such as sudoku, crosswords, a
novel or journaling, and do them in place of evening scrolling (Bell, 2026).
Nobody has studied the stack as a whole. Its individual pieces have been
studied, and the timings below come from those studies.

It is different from **habit stacking** (James Clear, building on BJ Fogg),
which means tying a new habit to an existing one: "after I brush my teeth, I
open Brain Stack." The closest evidence is research on if-then plans
(Sheeran et al., 2024), and cues tied to an existing routine worked as well as
fixed clock times (Keller et al., 2021).

## Recommended timings

| Activity | Default | Range | Key evidence | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Set the scene (dim lights, phone down) | 2 min | 1–2 | Keep evening light low for the 3 h before bed (Brown 2022) | Medium |
| Journal / brain dump | 5–10 min, open-ended | 3–10 | 5 min of writing a specific to-do list at bedtime: fell asleep in ~15 min vs ~25 min for people listing finished tasks (Scullin 2018; lab study, n=57, one night, no non-writing group) | Low–medium |
| Puzzle (paper, untimed, a little easy) | 10 min, firm stop | 5–20 | No cognitive gain from daily jigsaws (Fissler 2018, RCT n=100); little transfer from brain training (Simons 2016); 30 min of mental tasks right before bed delayed sleep onset (Wuyts 2012, n=15) | Low |
| Coloring (mandala / repeating pattern) | 15 min | 10–20 | 20 min of mandala coloring lowered anxiety more than free drawing (Curry 2005, n=84; van der Vennet 2012, n=50); 10 min/day for 7 days lowered anxiety and depressive symptoms (Flett 2017, n=104); a single 10-min session showed no difference (Mantzios 2018, n=84) | Medium (short-term anxiety) |
| Reading (print or e-ink, warm light) | 20 min | 10–30 | Reading in bed for 7 nights: 42% vs 28% reported better sleep (Finucane 2021, RCT n=991, self-reported, unblinded) | Medium |
| Three good things | 3 min | 2–5 | Nightly for 1 week: more happiness, fewer depressive symptoms through 6 months (Seligman 2005); meta-analysis g=−0.29, smaller vs active controls (Cregg 2021) | Low–medium |
| Breathing | 5 min | 2–10 | 5 min/day for 28 days: anxiety fell in every group; cyclic sighing beat mindfulness only on positive mood and resting breathing rate (Balban 2023, n=108); breathwork vs stress g=−0.35 (Fincham 2023) | Medium |
| Worry time (optional, early evening) | 10 min | 10–20 | Worry postponement: small cuts in worry duration (d=0.31) and frequency (d=0.19) across 7 RCTs (Dippel 2023); no sleep benefit (McCarrick 2025, n=186) | Medium for worry, low for sleep |
| Muscle relaxation / body scan (optional, in bed) | 10 min | 5–20 | Relaxation therapy has a conditional recommendation for insomnia (Edinger 2021); no standard length | Low–medium |

**About journaling without a time limit.** Classic expressive writing uses 15
minutes × 4 days, with a dose effect: sessions under 15 min r≈.01, 15 min or
longer r≈.08 (Frattaroli 2006). It works as an occasional course, not a nightly
habit, and people often feel worse right afterwards. For nightly use, concrete
"what is my next step?" writing is the helpful kind; abstract "why?" thinking
feeds rumination (Watkins 2008), and self-distanced prompts ("what would I
tell a friend?") reduce emotional intensity (g=0.52; Moran & Eyal 2022). So
Brain Stack keeps journaling open-ended, as requested, with a gentle nudge at
10 minutes and a "Park it" button instead of a hard stop.

## Order

No trial has compared orders. The logic is to move from mental effort to
bodily calm, because mental load right before bed delays sleep (Wuyts 2012):

1. **Set the scene**: dim lights, phone on Do Not Disturb, materials out.
2. **Journal / brain dump**: get open loops out of your head first.
3. **Puzzle**: the most demanding step; ends at least 30 min before lights-out.
4. **Coloring**: absorbing, low demand.
5. **Reading**: passive; move to bed or a comfy chair.
6. **Three good things**: a short positive close.
7. **Breathing**: leads straight into lights-out.

There's no evidence the routine has to end some time before bed. A worry
window, if you use one, belongs earlier in the evening, separate from this.

## The three routines in the app

| Routine | Steps | Total |
|---|---|---|
| Full | Scene 2 · Journal ~10 (open) · Puzzle 10 · Coloring 15 · Reading 20 · Three good things 3 · Breathe 5 | ~65 min |
| Everyday | Scene 1 · Journal ~5 (open) · Coloring 10 · Reading 15 · Breathe 4 | ~35 min |
| Low battery | Brain dump 3 · Reading 6 · Breathe 3 | ~12 min |

Everyday uses coloring rather than the puzzle: it has the better evidence, is
calmer, and there isn't room to finish a puzzle 30 minutes before the end.
On hard days, one written line plus a minute of breathing still counts.

## Breathing patterns

- **Cyclic sighing** (routine step): 3 s inhale through the nose, 1 s top-up
  inhale, 6 s exhale through the mouth. Balban gave no exact timings; these
  fall in the suggested 2–3 s / 1 s / 6–8 s range.
- **Slow breathing** (Quick calm): 4 s in, 6 s out, no holds. Longer exhales
  feel more relaxing (Van Diest 2014).
- **For panic**: slow, gentle, not deep, no breath holds, 1–3 minutes. Present
  it as comfort rather than treatment: breathing retraining added little to
  CBT for panic disorder (Pompoli 2018). No hyperventilation-style breathwork.
- **Relaxation can backfire**: 31–54% of tense people felt more tense during
  relaxation in one study (Heide & Borkovec 1983). Quick calm therefore offers
  a 5-4-3-2-1 grounding alternative, and every step can be stopped.

## How the research shaped the app (ADHD and anxiety)

- **Visual time**: a shrinking wedge plus the clock time each step ends.
  Timing deficits are documented in ADHD (Marx 2022), though adult data are
  mixed (Mette 2023); no adult trials of visual timers were found.
- **Soft transitions**: heads-up chime (2 min before steps of 10 min or more,
  1 min otherwise) naming the next step, an up-next screen, "+5 minutes".
  Based on practice rather than adult trials.
- **Hyperfocus guard**: puzzles have a firm stop (Hupfeld 2019; participants
  in Fissler's trial reported "craving" to puzzle). Pick puzzles a little
  easy: flow needs challenge to match skill (Fong 2015).
- **Rotating prompts**: boredom is strongly linked to ADHD (r=.40; Muris 2026),
  so the structure stays fixed while prompts rotate nightly.
- **No streaks or points**: broken streaks reduce later engagement, more so
  when people blame themselves (Silverman & Barasch 2023); gamification didn't
  improve depression apps (Six 2021). Instead, a weekly goal (default 5 of 7)
  with rest nights built in, since goals with some slack keep people going
  (Sharif & Shu 2017). No sleep scores (Baron 2017, "orthosomnia").
- **Missed nights are fine**: missing one night didn't materially affect habit
  formation (Lally 2010).
- **Optional reminders, habit cues first**: reminders can get in the way of a
  habit becoming automatic (Stawarz 2015), and text reminders didn't improve
  adherence in adults with ADHD (Nordby 2022).
- **The app is a light source**: ADHD is linked to a later body clock (Coogan
  2017), and unrestricted tablet use pushed bedtime ~30 min later (Chinoy
  2018). Warm dark UI, screen auto-dims during steps, audio cues so the phone
  can lie face down.

## Claims deliberately left out

- **"6 minutes of reading cuts stress by 68%."** Traces to a 2009 Mindlab
  International report; no peer-reviewed paper, sample or methods, and
  retellings disagree.
- **"Puzzles boost cognition or lower cortisol."** Little transfer; no cortisol
  trial found. The null jigsaw trial was funded by a puzzle maker.
- **"Cyclic sighing beats mindfulness for anxiety."** Anxiety fell equally; the
  study was exploratory and mostly Stanford students.
- **"66 (or 21) days to form a habit."** 66 is the median of modeled curves
  (range 18–254) from self-reported automaticity; 21 has no basis.
- **"E-readers wreck sleep."** Chang 2015 used 4 h of iPad reading (~10 min
  longer to fall asleep); 30 min on an iPad didn't change sleep onset
  (Grønli 2016). Print or e-ink is still the easy choice.
- **"Gratitude treats depression," "worry time fixes sleep."** Effects are
  small or null.

## Not verified

Confirmed only from secondary sources: session lengths in Eaton & Tieber 2017
and Holt 2019; Pennebaker & Beall's 15 min × 4 days; Borkovec's 30-minute worry
window; Flett 2017's 10 min/day (press coverage); Chang 2015's melatonin and
body-clock figures; Wuyts 2012's minute values; the Støre & Jakobsson 2022
mandala meta-analysis (results inaccessible).
